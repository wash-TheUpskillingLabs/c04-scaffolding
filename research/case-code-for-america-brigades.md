# Case: Code for America's Brigade network (2012 to 2023)

A national network of local volunteer civic-tech chapters that a national
nonprofit started, supported, tried to redesign, and then ended. Chosen as
the case closest to The Labs in shape: neighbors, evenings, civic projects,
a national organization behind local groups. It is also a case of an
ending, which teaches more than a typical program.

Researched Nov 2026 by a builder in residence, from public sources only.
People are described by role, not name. Quotes from the March 2020 video
come from auto-generated captions and are approximate; check them against
the audio before quoting them elsewhere.

## Who they are

- **Code for America** is a national nonprofit that works on making
  government services work well. It started the **Brigade program** in
  2012: a network of local volunteer chapters
  ([Technical.ly, 2023](https://technical.ly/civic-news/code-for-dc-rebrand-civic-tech-dc)).
- In March 2020 a chapter organizer described the network as "about
  25,000 active members across 85 different brigades" (Brigade 2.0 video,
  1:39). This is a speaker's figure, not an audited count.
- By the time it ended, reporting put the network at roughly 60 chapters
  ([StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/code-for-america-former-brigades-regroup/)).
- **Our region's chapter** was Code for DC, a Brigade from 2012. It is now
  Civic Tech DC
  ([Technical.ly](https://technical.ly/civic-news/code-for-dc-rebrand-civic-tech-dc),
  [Civic Tech DC history](https://civictechdc.netlify.app/history/)).
- The model also spread abroad as **Code for All** (2013), first run by
  Code for America and later member-run
  ([Code for America archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/dentonfracking/20141029232209/http://www.codeforamerica.org/about/international),
  [Code for All history](https://medium.com/code-for-all/how-this-thing-started-code-for-alls-journey-from-creation-to-future-visions-6d45fb8434)).

## What they tried

**The national model.** Local volunteer chapters, usually built around
weekly hack nights and events such as National Day of Civic Hacking, with
Code for America as the national umbrella and, for many chapters, fiscal
sponsor. *What structure the national organization actually gave a new
chapter (playbooks, staff, training) is not yet sourced; see
[How to Start a CFA Brigade](https://discourse.codeforamerica.org/t/how-to-start-a-cfa-brigade/199),
Jun 2018, not yet read.*

**What the larger chapters tried (Brigade 2.0 session, Mar 2020).** Four
of the network's largest chapters described moving away from the
all-volunteer model:

- **Code for KC** became a program of a local nonprofit with a core team
  of seven, one part-time staff member and a volunteer captain giving 10–15
  hours a week. Its lesson: "the need for dedicated capacity," a backbone
  organization (7:56–18:29; 16:40–18:15).
- **BetaNYC** moved away from weekly hack nights because people "were more
  interested in sustained engagement" than one-time events (22:53). It
  started a fellowship in 2015: a fall bootcamp for about 12 CUNY students,
  then spring projects; fellows can return as paid mentors (25:29, 34:00).
- **Code for Miami** shifted to be "less volunteer based and more practice
  based" (39:39), and "less on projects and less on volunteer work and
  more on programs" (40:52).
- **Hack for LA** moved from large events to weekly hack nights because
  big events had "no long-term lasting impact" (49:00). It has "a very
  loose governance structure, there's no requirements for participation"
  (59:38).

**How chapters onboarded and kept people (same session, Q&A).**

- Code for Miami: treat a newcomer like an acquisition: learn their
  interest at signup and give them a valuable task quickly; people drop
  off when they "feel like they're not getting a lot of value"
  (1:09:53–1:10:57).
- Open Oakland: after leadership became unstable, moved to annual
  elections and invited project leads to monthly leadership meetings as an
  "onboard ramp" (1:16:47).

**Code for America's own framing (2022).** The national organization
described the change as part of "refining our values and role in the
world of civic tech… The same is true for the Code for America Network"
([A New Vision, Mar 9, 2022](https://codeforamerica.org/news/a-new-vision-for-the-code-for-america-network/)).

## What happened

- In Mar 2020, chapter leaders said "there's no clear pathways that we see
  to grow beyond being just a volunteer organization" (2:51), and that
  sponsors were hard to find for a paid core team (2:59–3:22).
- Hack for LA raised $1,000 in 2020 and was losing its main space after
  June; its captain worked about 90 hours a week unpaid (53:38–54:26). A
  city official mentioned "revolving door volunteers" (57:52).
- Several chapters described a roughly three-month window of high
  commitment from a new volunteer (1:00:21; 1:04:30; 1:06:07).
- A DC chapter volunteer described burnout, "people disappearing," and
  funding entirely from donations (1:13:18).
- Code for DC went on hiatus in Jun 2022, before the national
  announcement ([Civic Tech DC history](https://civictechdc.netlify.app/history/)).
- In early 2023 Code for America ended the program and its fiscal
  sponsorship of about 60 chapters. Reported reasons: pandemic-era
  difficulty convening, falling volunteerism, and harder fundraising for
  the network ([StateScoop](https://statescoop.com/code-for-america-former-brigades-regroup/)).
  Some chapters called it a "death knell"; some moved to other fiscal
  sponsors or formed new umbrella groups (same source).
- Code for America said it was "committed to supporting Brigades through
  this 10-month-long process to gracefully and successfully transition to
  independence"
  ([Reflections on the Brigade Network's Next Chapter](https://codeforamerica.org/news/reflections-on-the-brigade-networks-next-chapter/)).

## Why I think it happened (the builder's read)

*This section is the researcher's interpretation, not a finding.*

"Maybe this is more about the volunteers. The demand for learning is
there. But there is still a structure problem. What are we offering and
for whom and by whom?"

- Moments that support this read: 22:53 (BetaNYC, sustained engagement),
  1:09:53 (Miami, give newcomers value fast), 7:56–18:29 and 16:40–18:15
  (KC, the need for a paid backbone).
- The builder reads Hack for LA at 55:14 as showing that people came to
  get better and more employable. The captain said: "many software
  volunteers are willing to trade their software development labor for
  becoming better at their craft, becoming more employable, giving back to
  the community, being part of the community" (55:14–55:29), and described
  the chapter's main impact as helping volunteers "get their first jobs in
  tech" (56:01–57:07). Limits: this is one chapter leader's description of
  "many software volunteers," it lists giving back and belonging alongside
  skills, and the captions are unchecked against the audio. The builder's
  read of those other two: giving back and belonging are further reasons
  for joining, but they don't say what people wanted to learn.
- "The demand for learning is there" also comes from the builder's own
  conversations with people in DC who wanted to learn and then joined a
  Build Cycle. That is evidence about The Labs, not about the Brigades.
  See [past participant, #16](interview-past-participant-2026-11-07.md),
  [maintainer self-report](interview-maintainer-self-report.md), and
  [past participant and Poderator, #29](interview-past-participant-poderator-2026-11-13.md).

- **Course or project (builder's read).** Of what Hack for LA lists at
  56:37–57:07 (substantial projects with real partners, a resume line,
  teamwork, Git flow), the builder's view: experience can come from a
  project or a cohort, and a course or workshop gives the skills to start,
  with the potential to get more involved through a project or Cycle.
- **Learning to get work (builder's read).** The builder sees a pattern
  across three sources: Hack for LA's first jobs in tech (56:01–57:07), a
  past participant at The Labs who came after time out of work
  ([#16](interview-past-participant-2026-11-07.md)), and job hunting in the
  [maintainer self-report](interview-maintainer-self-report.md). Three
  sources from two places; how widely it holds is untested.

## Other explanations

1. **Place, not structure.** A format that needs people in one room every
   week is hard in a big metro area. Hack for LA's three hack nights were
   "an hour-and-a-half commute from each other in traffic" (49:24–50:12);
   BetaNYC dropped weekly hack nights because "people are spread out all
   over" (28:38). The pod's own interviews raise the same issue at The
   Labs: a past participant rarely attended in person because of about
   two hours of travel round trip into DC, so a session "needs to be
   something really valuable"
   ([#16](interview-past-participant-2026-11-07.md)); the interviewer's
   notes on [#23](interview-c03-c04-participant-2026-11-09.md) say both
   interviewees so far live about an hour away in traffic and couldn't
   attend all workshops in person.
2. **The national organization changed course.** Code for America's 2022
   framing puts the change in its own "values and role," not in the
   chapters' troubles.
3. **The pandemic and the funding climate,** as reported in 2023. Against
   this: chapter leaders were already describing the sustainability
   problem in Feb–Mar 2020, before the pandemic's effects.

Which of these best fits, and what evidence would tell them apart, is
still open.

## What we can't tell from outside

- **Whether this still applies.** The chapter accounts are from 2020,
  before AI tools became mainstream. The builder notes that AI has since
  become something people feel they must learn, and that people fear being
  replaced or downsized because of it. The source for that is not yet
  named in this file.

- **Why people came to the Brigades, beyond one chapter leader's view.**
  The only source on this is Hack for LA's captain (see "Why I think it
  happened"). No volunteer speaks for themselves, and no other chapter
  says why people joined.
- **Whether people learned,** and how anyone would have known.
- **What structure the national organization gave chapters,** beyond
  fiscal sponsorship and national events.
- **How typical the four chapters in the video were.** They described
  themselves as among the 15 largest, so smaller chapters may differ.
- **Code for America's internal reasons** for the 2023 decision beyond
  public statements.
- **How 41:24 should be read.** Code for Miami said outsiders saw them as
  "a meetup or just a college CS group," and they treated that as a
  problem. The builder reads this as a funding problem: in the source it
  is the first of a list of lessons whose next items are funders confusing
  them with Code for America and the lack of a business model
  (41:24–42:36). Not read as evidence about learning. An alternative
  reading, not adopted: being seen as a learning space hurt the group's
  standing with partners.
