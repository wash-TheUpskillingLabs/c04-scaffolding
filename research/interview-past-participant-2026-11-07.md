# Interview: past participant on purpose, structure, the pod phase, and workshops

Filed as issue #16 (Nov 7, 2026). Her written Slack comments are in a comment on that issue.

1. What it is
A conversation with a participant from the pilot Build Cycle (Health Care)
and the Energy & Climate Cycle (on a recycling pod) about the Cycle's
purpose and structure: how much structure she needed, when it helped or
got in the way, and who provided it. Also covers guidance from organizers
and mentors, team formation, workshops, and what she learned.

2. Where it came from
A past participant returning to work after a long career break, with a
research and scientific-programming background. She has taught, and she is
now building her own educational software project with AI.

3. When
Nov 7, 2026 (about one hour, by video).

4. What it says
(Quotes are verbatim from the conversation, lightly trimmed with "...".)

Structure and judgment
- She first reacted on Slack to the C04 invite line "People handed a
  syllabus never build the judgment the Cycle is for," and disagreed with
  it. On the call she explained: "this idea that like, oh, if you provide
  structure, like... a syllabus, then people don't get the judgment, I
  just think it's not true."
- Drawing on her teaching: "Whenever you cannot clearly explain something
  or clearly teach it, most of the time, it's because the idea is not clear
  in your own head. Maybe the lab needs to make it clear in their own head
  what the purpose is."
  (From my notes taken during the call; the auto-transcript didn't capture this line clearly.)

Purpose and expectations
- "The lab needs to have a very clear message and that this is what was
  missing."
- On keeping the purpose open: "they are trying not to make this clear
  because they want to keep it widely open... They are coming from a good
  heart, good will, but by making it so open, you are going to get people
  with different needs, different purposes, and not everyone is there to
  have their own startup."
- On frameworks: design thinking makes "an assumption about what people
  want to learn... this whole design thinking and how to make a product,
  that's for somebody who wants to do a startup." When I said people inside
  companies use the same rapid-prototyping techniques for new systems and
  features: "That's true... you do make a point here. But... these skills,
  I believe people already have. What the upskilling lab is promising is
  something very specifically AI. At least that was my perception."
- On the Health Care Cycle's AI emphasis: "they were pushing like, No, you
  have to use AI for everything... it was like, really, like, grinding on
  my nerves... And then suddenly they switched."
- "It's good to get you started and then after that you just go on your
  own, but what they need to figure out is... who are your audience and
  what do they want."
- Suggested "encouraging people to also be clear in their own mind about
  what their goals are. So once everyone had a clear idea where they are
  heading, it becomes a little bit easier."
- Her own goal: "my main aim is to actually find a job and find my
  purpose. I did not reach this aim."
- On joining: "I have no idea what they are doing. But they seem to be like
  nice people... I'll just stick around and see how it goes... going in
  blindly going with the flow. But I did notice that everyone else was
  confused."
- "Most of the time, what I found is that when we get a meeting with the
  organizers, there are always this vague advice that people are still
  confused." Wanted "some best practices... something more tangible."

Timing of structure (pod phase)
- Organizers kept saying the group was "narrowing down too quickly" and
  "making assumptions," so "we kept on circling around the same one...
  different people are working on the same thing. So there is some efforts
  that are being replicated... which probably is not that bad since we're
  all learning."
- "This whole time, we've been trying to narrow it down and [they] keep
  telling us you're narrowing it too quickly. And suddenly, you have one
  week and like, now you need to narrow it... it seems like there's some
  conflicting messages."
- In the pilot, "two [pods] joined together into one project, and so it
  went the opposite of what they intended."
- What would help: "these are the tools you can use, and these are the
  expectations... this week is going to be for this purpose... more
  deadlines... and for people to actually meet and discuss the next step."
- Asked whether she knew where she was in the Cycle and what was due:
  "it's definitely better than previous cycles... a little bit more
  structure... This is what we want to research... These are the questions
  that we are asking... for this week, we are going to try to narrow it
  down. At least it makes it more clear what the expectations are."
  (My understanding: she was comparing the current "What to Expect"
  materials with her two Cycles. In her view, they've improved a little
  each time.)
- Were the assignments clear? "No, no, not exactly, but... we figured it
  out."
- "Each cycle, they change things." Felt she "worked harder on the first
  cycle."

Pod vs. team
- In the Energy & Climate Cycle, pod members were meeting and talking about
  projects, then: "you are too many here. You definitely are not going to
  be on the same project. You have to do something different. And that
  took people aback."
- "The other problem with switching from [pod] to project is that people
  feel that they don't know where to put their energy... during the pod,
  people don't put that much effort... because they are confused, they
  have no idea what's going on."
- When I suggested defining what a pod is versus a team: "maybe it's just
  making it very clear from the very beginning. It does help."
- Teams formed around people more than projects: "you end up really just,
  like, choosing the project where you just feel like, okay, I'm
  comfortable working with these people."
- Proposing a problem felt like being made its leader: "now everyone is
  expecting me to lead."

Who gives help
- Pilot Cycle, first week: "Complete silence on Slack." She started
  posting her research, and others joined because "oh, here's somebody
  actually posting here, so maybe I should engage here."
- In the Energy & Climate Cycle, ended up explaining the process to others:
  "hey, guys, I did this cycle before. This is how it normally goes. And
  even though that's not, you know, my job to explain things to you."
- On a teammate in the recycling pod acting like a founder: "he's acting
  there like he's the CEO and he's coming and he's looking for employees...
  I just felt that the lab should have stepped in, not me. To explain that
  this is not the purpose... that we are here to learn."
- A mentor (a volunteer, "taking... out of his own time") didn't answer
  questions about security technology and legal issues in the Health Care
  Cycle, and in a later Cycle told her to "ask the AI... that's not
  helpful." Experts help most for "a very specific question that I cannot
  find by myself... something that is based on experience."
- On subject-matter experts generally: "I'm sure a lot of people benefited
  from that. I didn't... It's just not the way I learn." Overheard that
  "some people did find it helpful."
- Poderators: "it's always good to have somebody who knows what's going on
  behind the scenes to be able to clarify to the participants."
- On people working alone: "how would I know that you are doing some work
  by yourself if you are not sharing it with me... we're all here to learn."
  Suggested "encouraging meetings... virtual... face-to-face... it starts
  the conversation."

Workshops and tools
- Were workshops helpful? "Honestly, not really. But again... I think
  probably for other people it was... it's either something I'm not
  interested in or I don't need, or something that, like, no, I have no
  idea."
- An agentic AI workshop assumed knowledge she didn't have ("I keep
  hearing this term, but I don't know what's like an agent") and was tied
  to one vendor's tool ("I'm not using copilot"). Wanted it "presented more
  like in levels, like step by step," and said: "For me, it's helpful to
  say like, okay, well, this is the definition. This is what it does. Okay,
  now here's a hands-on thing you do for this new information so that you
  get a feel for what that is. And then, okay, now let's go to the next
  information... this is the way I teach too."
  Recognized the trade-off: "if they are going to start from my level, for
  somebody else, it's going to be boring."
- When I suggested optional background material before workshops, she
  agreed (my idea, so weaker evidence) and added: "here's where you can get
  this information."
- GitHub: in the pilot, "many people on the team, they wanted to do coding
  and stuff. So I just like started the GitHub repo, but everybody was
  confused about it... I did not know how to explain it to them... it
  wasn't my job." Couldn't help across setups ("if somebody is using
  Windows... I don't know what to tell you"), so "these workshops are
  important... an introduction to GitHub... and do hands-on to get them
  started."
- The Lab recommended NotebookLM, and it was very useful for research, but
  it wasn't covered in a workshop or reading material: "they just said
  like, hey, you know, you need to use that."
- Would like "more technical workshops," but "not sure if that is
  appropriate for the upskilling labs because it's assuming people with no
  technical skills." When asked, suggested after the Cycle: "if you want to
  go a little bit further, here are some... extra workshops."
- Rarely attended in-person sessions: about two hours of travel round trip
  into DC, so "it needs to be something like really valuable." More virtual
  workshops would help.

What she learned
- To a hiring manager: "it made me more confident using AI... now I feel
  confident with AI that I can actually work on big projects or bigger
  projects, things that I've never done before... there are ways to make
  sure of the quality of what the AI is providing."
- Learned to work with AI on unfamiliar work (first front-end project):
  "how to do the prompts, how to check, how to do tests... I'm not going to
  look at every single line of code."
- Learned from teammates' small comments (developing code in layers,
  separating front end and back end): "I learned a few things here and
  there, even from the non-technical people."
- Most learning was self-directed: "I learned a lot, it's just because I
  just decided like, okay, you know what, this is what I want to learn."
- Found peer learning valuable. Besides teammates' technical comments
  (above), others in her cohort showed her that meetings can be
  transcribed: "I was like, oh, I'm going to write down, like, notes. And
  everybody was laughing at me because I'm like, yeah, that is like this AI
  transcribing." On connections generally: "it's definitely good, like, to
  have all these connections."
- On prototypes vs. real projects: "AI is easy. But as your project gets
  bigger and bigger, it becomes hard."
- Would not do another Cycle. The pod phase felt slow because she wanted
  to get to the coding. If starting over: "the way the first cycle was done.
  It wasn't bad... it's okay to have one week of like, just people trying to
  slowly dip their toes in the water."

5. What it tells us (my read)

Takeaway (what this interview suggests):
"Start from where you are." Scaffolding works when the Lab is clear about
its purpose and meets people at their level, and it falls apart when
guidance is vague or reverses partway through.

Key learning (my read, to be tested with others):
Scaffolding (support that helps people do what they can't yet do alone,
and fades as they grow) only works when participants can see what it's
for. Participants need a clear, upfront sense of the Cycle's purpose,
intended audience, and structure (what happens at each stage, what's
expected, and what support is available). Without it, the support,
terminology, and expectations feel misaligned.

Finding on materials and content (my read, from one interview, to test):
Learning content isn't labeled by level or prerequisite, so participants
can't tell what a session is for, whether it's for them, or where to catch
up, and the gaps fall to peers.

Workshops and learning materials weren't matched to where participants
were, and there was no way to tell in advance. Sessions either covered
things people already knew or assumed background they didn't have, with no
clear starting level, no prerequisites, and no pointer to where to catch
up. Tools the Cycle relied on (GitHub, NotebookLM) were recommended but not
taught. For GitHub, the gap fell to whichever participant happened to know it.

In her words:
- Not matched to where people were: "it's either something I'm not
  interested in or I don't need, or something that, like, no, I have no
  idea."
- Assumed background: "I keep hearing this term, but I don't know what's
  like an agent."
- No starting level: she wanted it "presented more like in levels, like
  step by step... this is the definition. This is what it does... here's a
  hands-on thing," while recognizing that "if they are going to start from
  my level, for somebody else, it's going to be boring."
- Nowhere to catch up: "here's where you can get this information."
- Recommended but not taught: on NotebookLM, "they just said like, hey,
  you know, you need to use that."
- The gap fell to peers: on GitHub, "I just like started the GitHub repo,
  but everybody was confused about it... it wasn't my job."

She pushed back on the idea that structure blocks judgment. At the start of
the call she said: "if you provide structure, like it's a syllabus, then
people don't get the judgment, I just think it's not true." Drawing on her
teaching, she said: "Whenever you cannot clearly explain something or
clearly teach it, most of the time, it's because the idea is not clear in
your own head. Maybe the lab needs to make it clear in their own head what
the purpose is."
(From my notes taken during the call; the auto-transcript didn't capture this line clearly.)

Purpose and frameworks: My read is that she was skeptical of frameworks as
a substitute for clarity about purpose. She said design thinking makes "an
assumption about what people want to learn," and that by keeping the
purpose "so open, you are going to get people with different needs... not
everyone is there to have their own startup." When I pointed out that
people inside companies use the same rapid-prototyping techniques for new
systems and features, she agreed ("you do make a point here"), but added:
"these skills, I believe people already have. What the upskilling lab is
promising is something very specifically AI. At least that was my
perception." That cuts against my idea that participants want their work
tied to recognized practices (design thinking, human-centered design,
continuous product development, rapid prototyping, lean startup, agile).
For her, the draw was AI. Worth testing in other interviews.

How much: I think she wanted clearer structure, not necessarily "more" of
it: due dates, and what type of work we might be doing this week ("this
week is going to be for this purpose... more deadlines"). Her examples
sort into three kinds:
- Structure she asked for: what each week is for, deadlines, which tools
  might be useful, and what's expected ("these are the tools you can use,
  and these are the expectations"). My read of "tools," from context: not
  just software, but methods and resources for working through a question:
  frameworks or approaches for figuring out the problem, research methods
  (e.g., user interviews), workshop exercises, ways to organize thinking,
  and AI tools such as NotebookLM. In other words, a few concrete ways to
  tackle the week's question without prescribing a single path. She also
  asked for regular meetings to discuss next steps, an intro GitHub
  workshop with hands-on practice, and workshops taught "in levels, like
  step by step... this is the definition. This is what it does... here's a
  hands-on thing." And she suggested a clearer statement of purpose and
  audience, and encouraging each participant to name their own goals.
- Structure that didn't help: general advice ("vague advice that people
  are still confused"), guidance that reversed ("narrowing down too
  quickly," then one week to narrow), a mandate that shifted ("you have to
  use AI for everything... And then suddenly they switched"), a workshop
  tied to one vendor's tool, and tools recommended without guidance
  (NotebookLM: she found the tool itself useful; my read is that the
  missing guidance was the gap, not the tool).
- Where she did without structure: her most valuable learning was
  self-directed ("I just decided like, okay, you know what, this is what I
  want to learn"), she did her own research, and she learned from
  teammates' small comments.
In my read, the structure she valued tells people what to do and when, and
builds up from the basics. The structure that failed was vague, reversed
partway through, or prescribed a tool without saying why.

When: My summary of what she described: the structure came too slow, then
too fast. Her complaint wasn't too much or too little structure in general.
It was that the timing of structure didn't match the work.
- Too slow in the pod phase. Her group wanted to narrow the problem, but
  organizers kept saying they were "narrowing down too quickly" and "making
  assumptions," so they kept circling and duplicated effort.
- Too fast at the move to projects: "suddenly, you have one week and like,
  now you need to narrow it... it seems like there's some conflicting
  messages."
In my read: early on, there wasn't enough clarity on direction; later,
there was too much pressure to decide fast. By clarity, she meant several
things:
- What each week is for: "this week is going to be for this purpose... more
  deadlines... and for people to actually meet and discuss the next step."
- Something concrete, not general advice: "some best practices...
  something more tangible," instead of "vague advice that people are still
  confused" by.
- Where to put effort before teams form: "people feel that they don't know
  where to put their energy."
- Purpose, for the Lab and for each participant: "make it very clear the
  purpose and the expectations," and help people "be clear in their own
  mind about what their goals are."

Sequence and labels: She said the recent structure was "definitely better
than previous cycles... at least it makes it more clear what the
expectations are." (My understanding: she was comparing the current "What
to Expect" materials with her two Cycles, which she felt have improved a
little each time.) The point about labels meaning different things to people
from different backgrounds is mostly my own experience (a former workplace
called pods "teams"); her version was that people come "from different
backgrounds" and "are at different levels."

Pod formation: In the Energy & Climate Cycle (on a recycling pod), people
in the pod were meeting and talking about projects together. Then it
became clear there were too many people for everyone to end up on the same
project ("you are too many here... that took people aback"). That caused
confusion and threw people off, and it made people unsure where to put
their effort during the pod phase. When I suggested defining what a pod is
versus a team, she agreed it should be "very clear from the very
beginning."
My read: people treated the pod as the team they would keep, and the Lab
didn't make clear early enough that a pod is not the final team. More
broadly, participants didn't know what was coming next. The split into
project teams came as a surprise because the sequence (pod, then smaller
project teams) wasn't laid out clearly enough.

From whom: She thinks the Lab should provide GitHub materials and an intro
workshop rather than leaving it to whichever participant knows GitHub, and
more advanced technical workshops, possibly after a Cycle. On a teammate
acting like a founder: "the lab should have stepped in, not me." The Lab
recommended NotebookLM, and she found it very useful for research, but it
wasn't covered in any workshop or reading material. The workshop that
missed for her did so for two reasons: it assumed background she didn't
have, and it was tied to one vendor's tool.

Finding on tools (my read): GitHub worked as an expectation without being
taught, so participants who already knew it filled the gap for others. She
already knew it ("I did not need that, I already knew how to work with
GitHub"), set up the repo for her team, and at times felt this support
should also have come from the Lab ("it wasn't my job"; "these workshops are
important... an introduction to GitHub... and do hands-on to get them
started").
Context: GitHub is used at The Labs and organizers want to keep using it
(from workshops with organizers); in the interview, we both took it as given.
Note (Nov 9, 2026): The Labs' events calendar (Luma) lists GitHub
workshops from May 2026: "Introduction to GitHub for Team Projects" (May 16,
Aug 18) and "GitHub 2.0: The Handoff" (Jun 16, Sep 22), during the Energy &
Climate and Civics & Elections Cycles. Her GitHub story is from the pilot
Cycle, so "without being taught" (here and in the finding on materials)
may describe the pilot rather than later Cycles.

My own experience: What she came to learn was different from me. I
specifically wanted to learn to vibe code. She came to get her bearings
and find her way back to work ("my main aim is to actually find a job and
find my purpose"), with AI as the draw ("something very specifically AI")
and coding as the part she enjoyed ("for me, I wanted to do the coding
part"). I came for a specific skill; she came for direction and wanted to
use the Cycle as a way back in. She learned useful skills, enough to start
building her own educational software project, but hasn't yet found her
way back to work ("I did not reach this aim").

What surprised me: The main issue wasn't that participants needed more
or less structure overall. Structure only helps when its purpose, timing,
and level are clear. For this participant, simple, step-by-step
scaffolding and optional background materials would have been more useful,
in her view, than broad workshops or generic mentor advice ("ask the AI").
- She said workshops often weren't helpful for her because they were
  tied to one vendor's tool, assumed background she didn't have, or
  covered things she didn't need.
- How strongly she tied the Lab's problems back to clarity of purpose and
  audience.
- How self-directed she was, and how much she learned on her own.
- In a workshop, a clearer sequence mattered more than more content:
  definition, then what it does, then a hands-on step, then the next
  topic.

What resonated with earlier pod discussions (a signal of validation, with
a caveat): she wanted to be able to review the basics ahead of time (my
inference: so the live session could focus on the actual work), and she
wanted workshops to respect different starting points ("start from where
you are"). One-size-fits-all workshops can miss both beginners and
advanced participants unless there's some level-setting. Caveat: the
background-material idea came from me during the call, so her agreement
is partly my own idea coming back. The need behind it (assumed knowledge,
wanting levels) was hers, unprompted.

How sure I am: This is one person, and she said her case may be an
exception. She came to get her bearings after a long time out of work,
already knew how to do research, and often didn't need the workshops or
mentors. So her self-taught path may not be typical. But she also said "I
did notice that everyone else was confused," so the confusion about
purpose and expectations may be.

To believe it's a pattern, I'd need to hear the same two things from
others, unprompted:
1. The key learning above: that participants need a clear, upfront sense
   of the Cycle's purpose, intended audience, and structure (what happens
   at each stage, what's expected, and what support is available), or else
   the support, terminology, and expectations feel misaligned.
2. That participants want structure about what and when (what each week is
   for, what's due, what comes next, what tools might help), even if they
   want freedom in how they do the work.
I'd want to hear this from at least 2-3 other past participants, including
someone from a different background, and see it confirmed from the other
side by someone involved in running the Cycle (e.g., a Poderator, an
organizer, and/or a mentor). I'd also look for the opposite: anyone who
found the Cycle clear enough, or wanted less structure.

7. What research is still needed
- More past participants, to understand their perceptions and goals: why
  they joined, what they wanted to learn (AI? recognized practices like
  design thinking, human-centered design, continuous product development,
  rapid prototyping, lean startup or agile? a job? a startup?), and
  whether they shared her confusion about purpose, the pod phase, and what came next. Ideally
  people who differ from her: newer to tech, newer to The Labs, or still
  active.
- Other stakeholders, to hear the other side of the same moments:
  - Poderators: how they saw the pod-to-project transition and where pods
    got stuck
  - Mentors and subject-matter experts: what they get asked, how much time
    they have, and what "ask the AI" looks like from their side
  - Workshop leaders: how they decide what level to teach at
  - Organizers / steering committee: what the purpose and the pod-to-team
    sequence were meant to be, and why guidance changed between Cycles
  - Employers or hiring managers: which skills and labels they recognize
- A follow-up with this participant on one question I didn't ask: "Was
  there ever something you needed that you couldn't find, or didn't know
  existed?"

8. Ideas parked for later (mine, not evidence; for frames after the paradox)
- It might help to list what evidence each step should show (interviews,
  stakeholder feedback) and leave the how open (user research, desk
  research, whatever the problem needs).
- Breaking the process into smaller, clearly named stages (the problem,
  narrowing it, this week's questions, what to do and with which tools)
  would help people know what stage they're in and what comes next.
- Modules on lightweight QA for AI-assisted work could help (from
  takeaway 4).
