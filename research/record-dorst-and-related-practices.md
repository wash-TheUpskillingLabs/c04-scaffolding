# Record: Connecting Dorst's frame creation to human-centered design and related practices


Filed as issue #20.
1. What it is
The Build Cycle is built on Kees Dorst's frame creation (archaeology,
paradox, context, field, themes, frames, futures, transformation,
integration), a method for reframing hard problems. It also draws on other methods: work
is organized in sprints, ideas are tested with rapid prototypes, and
projects ship in weekly releases. The materials teach the steps in Dorst's
own terms. That creates an opportunity: showing participants how the
Build Cycle combines these, and how each step relates to human-centered
design and related practices employers know, such as design thinking,
continuous product development, rapid prototyping, lean startup and agile.

4. What it says
- The C04 READMEs present Dorst's nine steps by name and quote his definitions.
- The Civics & Elections guide introduces Dorst's method as optional depth: "You don't
  *have* to be familiar with it."
- The Civics & Elections value proposition promises "durable skills" including
  "collaboration, stakeholder engagement, user research, storytelling."
  Those skills aren't yet linked to the step names participants use day to
  day.
- At the Aug 15 hackathon, a Design Thinking workshop was offered on the
  Newcomer track.
- The Build Cycle also uses methods from other traditions, without naming
  where they come from: sprints (the Sensemaking Sprint, the hackathon's
  "Pod Sprint" track), rapid prototypes "put in front of three real
  people" (Scaffolding README), and weekly releases "to real people" (C04
  README). A maintainer didn't realize the Cycle was organized in sprints
  until after it ended.
- "Scaffolding," the pod's own name, isn't yet defined in the README.

5. What it tells us (my read)
As a participant, I couldn't easily say how what we did related back to
human-centered design or design thinking, and it seemed to me like
others in my pod and project team felt the same (my observation). The method we used has a name and a structure, but the names we were
given (archaeology, paradox, frames) aren't ones I was already familiar
with, and aren't ones I could reference in a job interview or at work
without explaining them first.

The Labs already promises "durable skills" like user research and
stakeholder engagement. The skills are there. The opportunity is in the
terminology, and in connecting what participants do to overarching and
complementary principles like human-centered design and design thinking.
Participants could relate what they're learning, at least loosely, to
human-centered design and related practices: design thinking, continuous
product development, rapid prototyping, lean startup, agile.

How the Build Cycle combines them (my read): Dorst's frame creation
supplies the steps (what to work on), sprints and releases supply the
rhythm (when, in what time-boxes), and methods like rapid prototyping
supply the techniques (how), all guided by the principle of human-centered
design. Participants experience all of these but mostly hear only Dorst's
names. Showing how they fit together, and whether to name the other
methods explicitly, is part of the opportunity.

What I don't know yet: whether participants see this as important, or
whether it matters more to me than to them. One past participant
interviewed on Nov 7 pushed against it: she said these are skills "people
already have" and that the Labs promises "something very specifically AI."
That's one person, so it needs testing in more interviews.

6. Is any of this hearsay?
No. All from documents, plus one interview (logged separately). The
crosswalk below was checked against Dorst & Kokotovich (2015), the Design
Council's Double Diamond, and the Stanford d.school's design thinking
modes. On Nov 10, 2026, it was rebased on the frameworks the Labs itself
points to: the Cycle 3 guide "Guidance for Project Research, Design &
Getting to Development" lists the Double Diamond and IDEO's design thinking
resources as "Frameworks We Like" (#24). The IDEO column is a draft, mapped
from IDEO U's six phases (frame a question, gather inspiration, generate
ideas, make ideas tangible, test to learn, share the story), and still
needs checking. The d.school's five modes moved to Complementary practices.
The Cycle 3 guide also has its own ten-step "Design Thinking Framework." Its
source isn't stated, and it appears to be a blend of the frameworks it links
to, so the crosswalk uses only named frameworks. The practices are related to Dorst's steps, not one-to-one
equivalents; the "Fit" column shows how close each match is. "Common
methods and tools" are techniques all of these approaches use at that
step; "complementary practices" are separate practices (lean startup,
agile, continuous product development) that build on frame creation,
mostly from Futures onward. The plain-language descriptions are summaries
of Dorst & Kokotovich's own one-line definitions.

Crosswalk: Dorst's steps and related practices (rough guide, not equivalents)

How these frameworks differ (my read). Dorst's frame creation spends
significant time on the problem before moving to solutions. Expert
designers "spend an awful lot of time... looking at their problem... once
they've got a new frame, the solutions follow very quickly" (Kees Dorst,
interviewed in the Design Theory and Methodology podcast, TU Delft, episode
1, "Reflective Practice"). He argues that interest in "design thinking"
"mostly focuses on the designer's abilities in generating solutions, rather
than on the key ability of expert designers to create new approaches to
problem situations ('framing')" (Frame Innovation, ch. 1). So this
crosswalk is a rough guide. IDEO's phases and the Double Diamond have no
named stage for much of Dorst's problem-side work, especially Archaeology
and Themes.

Human-centered design is the principle behind all of these: design around
the needs and context of the people affected. It has no steps of its own,
so it doesn't appear as a column, but it runs through Context, Field and
Futures in particular.

| Dorst step | What it means | Double Diamond | IDEO design thinking phases (draft) | Common methods and tools at this step | Complementary practices | Fit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Archaeology | Dig into the problem's history: who first called it a problem, how they described it, and what's already been tried | Discover | Gather inspiration (partly) | Desk research, problem history, review of past attempts, interviews about what happened | d.school: (no direct match) | Moderate |
| 2. Paradox | Name what makes the problem hard: two things that are both true and pull against each other | Discover to Define | Frame a question (partly) | Problem analysis, problem statement | d.school: Define (partly) | Partial |
| 3. Context | Map the inner circle: the people who affect the problem today | Discover | Gather inspiration | Stakeholder mapping, interviews, observation | d.school: Empathize | Good |
| 4. Field | Map the wider world around the problem: other players, their interests and values | Discover | Gather inspiration (wider) | Ecosystem or landscape mapping, market research | d.school: Empathize (wider) | Good |
| 5. Themes | Find the deeper motivations that keep showing up across those players | Define | (no named phase; between Gather inspiration and Generate ideas) | Synthesis, affinity mapping, insights | d.school: Define | Good |
| 6. Frames | Use patterns in the themes to see the problem a new way ("If we treat this as if it were...") | Define (reframing) | Frame a question (reframing); Generate ideas | Reframing, "How might we" questions, hypotheses | d.school: Define to Ideate | Good |
| 7. Futures | Explore what could happen under the new frame, and what's in it for each stakeholder | Develop | Generate ideas; Make ideas tangible; Test to learn | Rapid prototyping, user testing, value propositions | Lean startup (build-measure-learn, MVP); d.school: Ideate, Prototype, Test | Good |
| 8. Transformation | Work out what people and organizations would need to change to make it real | Deliver | Share the story (partly) | Implementation planning, change management, releases | Agile, continuous product development (in the Build Cycle: weekly releases); d.school: (beyond the five modes) | Partial |
| 9. Integration | Draw lessons from the new approach and spot new opportunities across the network | (after Deliver) | Share the story (partly) | Lessons learned, scaling, knowledge sharing | Agile retrospectives; d.school: (beyond the five modes) | Partial |

Sensemaking (the Labs' Sensemaking Sprint or sensemaking day) spans steps 1
to 5 in a single quick, intuitive pass: Discover into Define in the Double
Diamond, Gather inspiration and Frame a question in IDEO's phases
(Empathize into Define in the d.school modes). Dorst's steps then do
the same work with evidence. In design research, sensemaking is the core of
synthesis (Kolko 2010). The general idea, how people make meaning from an
ambiguous situation, comes from Weick (1995).
