## Who they are

The Wikimedia Foundation hosts Wikipedia and its sister projects. Content
is written and maintained by volunteer editors. Topic groups of editors
("WikiProjects") rate and improve articles in their area.

## Angle 3: how content is built bottom-up (what they tried)

**Articles grow by stages, rated by volunteers.** (Search summaries;
Wikipedia's own help pages.)
- A quality scale: Stub ("very short pages"), Start ("some meaningful
  content but... weak in many areas"), C, B ("mostly complete and does not
  have major problems"), then Good Article and Featured Article.
- Who rates: WikiProjects rate most classes. Good Article status is
  "generally reviewed by a single independent editor after a nomination";
  Featured Articles "are reviewed by several editors."
- Projects may adapt the scale to their subject or skip tiers when they
  lack "an assessment team." The lower classes are "fairly subjective."
- Sources: [Content assessment](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:1.0/A),
  [Assessing articles](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:ARTASS),
  [Help: Assessing article quality](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Help:Assessing_article_quality),
  [WikiProject](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/WikiProject),
  [How to write a featured article](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:How_to_write_a_featured_article)

**To look for when reading:** how an article's first version is treated;
who can rate and how they learn to; how the scale compares with the module
catalog's `status` (draft, stable) and `reviewed_by`.

## Angle 3: what happened

- Sourcing improved: fewer unreliable sources and fewer sentences missing
  citations, 2010 to 2020
  ([arXiv, 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.05227.pdf)). Reputation
  improved by the late 2010s (secondary). No single study tracks factual
  accuracy since 2007.
- No research found yet on how articles move up the quality scale over
  time.

## Angle 2: volunteer mentors for newcomers (what they tried)

The Growth team's features (search summaries; design pages mostly
2019–2021, so current features may differ):
- A **newcomer homepage** with suggested tasks, and **a personal volunteer
  mentor** assigned to each newcomer, whose questions go to that mentor.
- Mentors enroll themselves on their home wiki; communities can limit
  enrollment to trusted users. Recruitment is left to each community;
  setup guidance says "you have to find some other mentors to volunteer,"
  and a team presentation suggested showing "how easy and not overbearing
  mentorship is."
- A **mentor dashboard** shows unanswered questions from mentees and
  mentees' activity, and a module meant "to show mentors that their efforts
  are making an impact." Mentors can quit; inactive mentors can be removed,
  with mentees reassigned.
- Sources: [Help: Mentorship](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Help:Growth/Mentorship),
  [mentor list setup](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Growth/Communities/How_to_configure_the_mentors%27_list),
  [structured mentor list](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Growth/Structured_mentor_list)

## Angle 2: what happened

- Experiments across 27 wikis: the newcomer homepage raised the share of
  newcomers making a first edit, but had no effect on whether they stayed
  ([homepage study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.09642),
  [research newsletter](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:Newsletter/2024/March)).
  A 2020 analysis of four wikis found a small retention gain via first
  edits ([Growth analysis](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Growth/Personalized_first_day/Newcomer_tasks/Experiment_analysis,_November_2020));
  its pages conflict on this.
- Mentorship has not been tested on its own. A 2025 survey called results
  "promising" ([survey](https://meta.wikimedia.org/wiki/Research:Successful_Newcomers_Survey_2025));
  a paper asks who benefits
  ([mentorship paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.11083)).
