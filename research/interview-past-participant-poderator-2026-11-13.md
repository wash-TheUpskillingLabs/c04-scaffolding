# Interview: past participant (Cycle 1) and Cycle 2 Poderator on team roles, the Poderator role, workshops and merging code

Filed as issue #29 (Nov 14, 2026).

1. What it is
A conversation about his experience of structure in the Cycles: how his
team worked, what he did when stuck, the Poderator role, workshops, and
when things felt clear.

2. Where it came from
A past participant (Cycle 1) who was a Poderator in Cycle 2 and now holds
the mentor recruiting and stewarding role. A maintainer's read: that role
is involved with Governance, specifically with mentors (not confirmed).

How he differs from earlier interviewees: he was on the support side
(Poderator) as well as a participant, he has a background in running
product and in teaching, and his Cycle (1) is earlier than #23 and #26.

3. When
Nov 13, 2026 (about 50 minutes, by video). Speaker labels in the
transcript were checked; the meeting tool's summary overstated several
points (for example, it listed his proposals as "decisions") and was not
used as a source.

4. What it says
(Quotes are verbatim from the transcript, lightly trimmed with "...", with
filler words such as "like" and "you know" removed where they don't change
the meaning. Speaker labels checked against the transcript; the Zoom summary
was not used as a source.)

How he came in
- He came looking for his "next opportunity" and heard about The Labs from a
  job-search group, where a participant from "cycle zero" posted about it.
  The pitch he heard: "you actually get to do hands-on work and have
  something to show for your time other than a certificate."
- His first contact was a workshop, "probably... something around better
  prompting," around February. "I attended the first workshop, and I
  thought, this is cool. I want to do this."

The start of Cycle 1: problem statements and voting
- "The very first thing that we were asked to do... Coming up with problem
  statements." Within about a week they were voting, "the somewhat confusing
  process of, it's got to have X number of votes in order to survive the
  next round, and which of these are actually the same problem statement."
- The problem he proposed (health care) lost people "one by one... there
  was a pretty finite exploration that could be done." He asked to be put
  on any team that needed someone: "they stuck me on a team."

On the team: no roles, no leader
- "A lot of the time after that was kind of an unstructured mess."
- What other teams said too: "it's week 2, it's week 3. I see the slide
  that tells me what phase we're in, but what work are we meant to be
  doing?" The answer they got: "that's part of figuring it out."
- Questions nobody answered: "is it okay for somebody to be a leader, or is
  this supposed to be a totally equal group? Are we supposed to have a
  project manager?... But didn't you say we should try to take on things
  that we hadn't done before? But the only person that's experienced in
  project management is experienced because they've done it before."
- What to build: "Are we vibe coding something? We could do a white paper.
  ... But how do we have multiple people working on vibe coding? Who's,
  quote unquote, in charge of the design?"
- "There was a lot of extremely inefficient thrashing while the group tried
  to figure out just how to govern itself. But we weren't there to learn
  how to govern small group interaction. We were there to do hands-on
  learning." Two or three meetings went that way; "people feeling less
  engaged, and then at least one member sort of didn't show up anymore...
  We didn't know who was setting the agenda."
- Why no one stepped up: "Even though we were getting direction that you
  have all the permission you need... We had a group of pretty empathic
  people... No one wanted to be perceived as taking over." "We talked about
  it afterwards. Everyone felt hesitant to do that." "We just floundered
  without direction for a long time."

Not knowing what to work on
- How often: "at least 50% of the time. And maybe more like 70."
- What they did: two of them would get on a call, "I'm feeling stuck
  again," and decide on a next step. He suggested a product requirements
  document (he "used to run product"); the rest of the team agreed.

Using AI together, not alone
- Writing the PRD: "This was the first time I had gotten onto a Zoom call
  with another human being. And the two of us let Claude ask us questions,
  we discussed, and then dropped the answers into Claude." "It was far
  better work than I would have done on my own. And he said the same...
  It was the best part of the whole cycle for us."
- On asking AI alone when stuck: "For us, I don't think that worked. I
  think it just gave us a bunch of different... flat options. Here's a list
  of 8 things that you can do... I don't know which to pick." "I definitely
  don't think that asking AI to get you unstuck is always the answer."
  (He had heard this advice, "maybe" from an organizer. Hearsay; see
  section 6.)

Getting unstuck: merging code across a team
- What the team wanted to learn decided what they built: "When the group
  asked each other how many people want to vibe code, everybody raised
  their hand."
- "How do we chunk up the development of an app across multiple people? And
  then, how do we bring that work together once it's been independently
  developed?" They knew it was "a totally normal problem" in software.
- He asked two mentors. One "didn't get back to us"; the other said "I'm
  gonna try to get you an answer... and I don't think we got one. I don't
  remember what the issue was, we just didn't hear anything from the
  mentors." (His recollection; he wasn't sure which mentors.)
- A teammate with a software background researched it on her own: "I've
  been researching this for the past week. I'm 90% sure I know how to do
  it." She told him she spent "like, 20-some hours." The output: "Merged
  code." "The rest of us have no idea what she did." Whether it used
  GitHub: "To this day, I don't know." When she explained it, "she lost me
  by, like, the third or fourth sentence."

The Poderator role (Cycle 2)
- "The Poderator role is definitely meant to get you unstuck," but among
  Poderators the question was "how much I'm, quote unquote, allowed?
  Encouraged? to... suggest?... [and] still preserves all of their...
  independence and opportunity for discovery, because that's what we
  thought we were meant to solve for." "We wound up feeling... very at sea
  around how much we should suggest, or had permission to suggest."
- "The definition around that role. There was so little of it. It was the
  first time we had done it... I think it didn't wind up doing much good."
- He didn't serve in Cycle 3 and doesn't know if the role changed.
- Two Poderators he recruited, one from each project he was Poderator
  for, in informal talks:
  "they felt like there was still a pretty significant gap in the structure
  of the cycle and therefore... how they felt comfortable intervening." And:
  "I think that [they] got the same direction I did, or at least
  internalized it in the same way, that less intervention was more... It
  wasn't clear what was appropriate or available." (Hearsay; see section 6.)
- His account of an organizer's position: "The more autonomy, the better.
  This should really be about discovery."

Workshops
- He went to "several." Presenters had "a lot of subject matter expertise,
  but not so much teaching expertise. And so the way they were scaffolding
  the content did not necessarily make it easy to understand." At least one
  deck "looks like the deck was built by Claude."
- "They were all kind, thoughtful, smart, capable people... I just didn't
  come away feeling like I had learned a lot... I didn't feel like I had
  had an opportunity to apply much of it, if any of it, in class, and
  therefore some amount of what I had learned only stayed in notes and not
  in my head."
- On prerequisites for attendees: "very challenging... Even pre-reading? You
  might get a third of the class. And so then you'd wind up covering the
  material anyway." (His estimate from his own teaching, not Labs data.)
- One workshop, on branding, was good, but "the reason that they chose it
  might have been because we had a much higher percentage of out of work
  attendees." He added, "now that we've decided that we're not going to
  focus so much on that target audience," branding seems "a little less
  relevant." (His account of a decision; no source. See section 6.)
- Mentors and presenters: "I'm not going to be able to find the right
  mentors if I don't know what they're supposed to be teaching." For
  workshops: "I love teaching storytelling. Is it necessarily the right fit
  for the 12-week cohort?... I'm not [certain]... without that path laid
  out and better defined, it's really hard to figure out what topics they
  need, at what point."

When things were clear
- "What are we meant to do for our presentations?... Why don't you take a
  look at one of the better decks from the last cycle?... It was a
  deliverable in which the contents of the deliverable were enumerated."
- In order: "Meet the Projects was the most clear." The Summit deck was
  less clear because of the audience: "who is our audience? I don't think
  that that was super clear. But still, we could go to the previous decks."
  Meet the Pods: "Still not clear really what the whole thing was about.
  Let alone, are we trying to recruit people?"
- What was clear: "all of the good intentions... the kind hearts, the
  integrity, the authenticity... it came through easily and frequently...
  [it] served as a stabilizer for a lot of our casting about, and as a
  buffer for a lot of our frustration."

The word "curriculum"
- "I know that there are folks that are concerned about the use of the word
  curriculum. My bias is that whether we call it a curriculum or an
  experiential journey or a learning journey," he'd want "a designed path
  ... the arc is, by design, intended to scaffold learning, so that you're
  building on what you've learned before."

At the close
- His ideas for more structure (below) haven't gotten traction. Responding
  to the interviewer's summary of themes, he said: "I'm really hopeful
  that this lands. I have had this conversation with several people. And
  I have not felt much enthusiasm... I don't know what the resistance is."
- The ideas he described: grouping people by what they want to build,
  with a recommended set of deliverables; three tiers of structure
  (decided for the team, optional to reach for, and the team's own); a
  short governance guide for small teams; a clearer Poderator role; and a
  designed learning path, whatever it's called. He named one he had
  discussed with an organizer: "an idea I talked to [an organizer] about
  is... group people by similar interests around what they wanted to
  build." Which of the others he raised, and with whom, he didn't say.

Ideas parked for later (his, not evidence; for frames after the paradox)
- Three tiers. Some things "we'll decide for you" (for example, small group
  roles) "so that the program starts and builds momentum quickly"; some
  things everyone should experience; and "stuff in the middle that we could
  offer... if they wind up starting to thrash, then they know it's
  available for them to reach for." The source: a story about a "canvas...
  [with] clear boundaries."
- Group people by what they want to build ("an app... a white paper... a
  podcast") rather than by problem statement, with a recommended set of
  about six deliverables. For a product: a design thinking canvas, problem
  statements, a published survey, a PRD, an app, a deck.
- A governance guide: "Here's a way to effectively run a small group for
  those of you who have been individual contributors... For me, I think a
  downloadable document would be fine."
- Poderators for "translation and sensemaking... pointers to resources. And
  as tier one technical support... shorter, more efficient conversations,
  probably more frequent."
- In his mentor recruiting and stewarding role, he plans to ask presenters
  for: what the audience needs to know, "three key points," and "at least
  two... in-class exercises, one of them to do independently, and one of
  them to do with other folks." "I sound like I have certainty around this.
  I don't... Whether it's the right approach for AI, I don't yet know."
- Presenters research what the audience needs and is stuck on, and bring
  examples for it: "better research by the mentors on what types of
  problems are you having, let me come prepared to give you an example of
  how to solve that particular problem," and "did you figure out what
  people need to know? Specifically, the people in the audience."
- Workshops as "general" (community-facing, could run "6 times a year" and
  invite people into the next Cycle) and "targeted" ("fits into the cycle
  for a specific reason at a specific point in time"). Deliverable
  workshops: "better presentations through AI, better PRDs through AI."
- Mentors as two roles: "SME on tap" (asynchronous) and presenter/teacher;
  "It could be the same person."

Interviewer's suggestions (kept separate)
- "That might be a good recommended approach to have Claude ask questions
  and to have more than one person there talking through requirements."
- Near the end, the interviewer described themes so far (a data model and
  taxonomy; more definition for the Poderator and project team tracks;
  modular learning; GitHub for an individual, then for a team) and noted
  "canvas" as an idea for a frame.
